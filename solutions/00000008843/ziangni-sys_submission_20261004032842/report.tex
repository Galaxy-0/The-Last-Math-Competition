\paragraph{Verdict.} The unqualified uniqueness assertion is false. The distinct closed convex functions $f(x)=0$ and $g(x)=1$ on $\mathbb R$ have identical subdifferentials at every point. The other maximal-monotonicity and cyclic-monotonicity clauses are not needed for this disproof.

\paragraph{Original claim and scope.} Both the English and Chinese statements assert that closed convex functions are uniquely determined by their subdifferentials. Neither supplies a normalization at one point nor an ``up to an additive constant'' qualification. We address the stated equality of functions, using everywhere finite, proper closed convex functions on the real line.

\paragraph{The actual subgradient definition.} For a real-valued convex function $h:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$, the subdifferential at $x$ is
\[
\partial h(x)=\{v\in\mathbb R:
  h(x)+v(y-x)\leq h(y)\ \text{for every }y\in\mathbb R\}.
\]
This is the usual global supporting-affine-function definition. The real dual space is identified with $\mathbb R$ by the linear functional $t\mapsto vt$; thus it is exactly the ordinary subdifferential in dimension one, not an assigned list of slopes.

\paragraph{Closedness and convexity.} For every real constant $c$, put $h_c(x)=c$. For any $x,y\in\mathbb R$ and $a,b\geq0$ with $a+b=1$,
\[
h_c(ax+by)=c=ac+bc=ah_c(x)+bh_c(y),
\]
so $h_c$ is convex. Its epigraph is the genuine closed half-space
\[
\operatorname{epi}(h_c)=\{(x,t)\in\mathbb R^2:c\leq t\}.
\]
Indeed, the coordinate projection $(x,t)\mapsto t$ is continuous and $[c,\infty)$ is closed. Thus $h_c$ is a closed convex function. It is everywhere finite and never takes $-\infty$, so it also meets the usual properness requirement when closed convex functions are treated as extended-real functions.

\paragraph{Computing every subgradient.} If $v\in\partial h_c(x)$, the defining inequality at $y=x+1$ gives $c+v\leq c$, hence $v\leq0$. At $y=x-1$ it gives $c-v\leq c$, hence $v\geq0$. Therefore $v=0$. Conversely $v=0$ satisfies the inequality for every real $y$. Consequently
\[
\partial h_c(x)=\{0\}\qquad\text{for all }c,x\in\mathbb R.
\]
Applying this to $c=0$ and $c=1$ proves $\partial f(x)=\partial g(x)$ at every point. But $f(0)=0\ne1=g(0)$, so $f\ne g$. This contradicts the universal uniqueness clause.

\paragraph{Why normalization matters.} More generally, for any real-valued function $h$ and real constant $c$, the global inequalities
\[
(h(x)+c)+v(y-x)\leq h(y)+c
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
h(x)+v(y-x)\leq h(y)
\]
are equivalent by cancellation. Hence $\partial(h+c)(x)=\partial h(x)$ for every $x$. The counterexample is intrinsic to the subdifferential definition. A uniqueness theorem with a suitable additive-constant qualification would be a different statement; the source does not include one.

The final theorem negates the universal unique-determination implication for closed convex real functions. This one-dimensional instance suffices to refute the unrestricted statement. No gradient table is supplied as an assumption, and no differentiability theorem or extra regularity hypothesis is needed.
