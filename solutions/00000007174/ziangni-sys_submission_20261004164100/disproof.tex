The stated antisymmetric-norm lower bound fails even for an invertible
normal real matrix. We use the standard spectral (Euclidean operator)
condition number $\kappa_2(A)=\|A\|_2\|A^{-1}\|_2$ and also check the
ratio of eigenvalue moduli, covering the source's spectral-ratio wording.

On the actual real Euclidean plane let
\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&-2\\2&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B=\begin{pmatrix}0&1/2\\-1/2&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Direct multiplication gives $BA=AB=I$, so $B=A^{-1}$.
For every vector $v=(x,y)$,
\[
Av=(-2y,2x),\quad \|Av\|_2=2\|v\|_2,
\qquad \|Bv\|_2=\tfrac12\|v\|_2.
\]
Taking the supremum over unit vectors therefore gives the actual
operator norms $\|A\|_2=2$ and $\|B\|_2=1/2$. Consequently
\[
\kappa_2(A)=1.
\]
The adjoint for the standard Euclidean inner product is $A^*=-A$.
In particular $A^*A=AA^*$, so $A$ is normal. Its standard antisymmetric
part is
\[
K=\frac{A-A^*}{2}=A,\qquad \|K\|_2=2>1=\kappa_2(A).
\]
Thus the norm of the antisymmetric part is not a lower bound for the
spectral condition number. If ``antisymmetric'' instead denotes the
unnormalized difference $A-A^*$, its norm is $4$, so that convention
also fails.

For completeness, the complexified matrix has characteristic equation
\[
\det(A-zI)=z^2+4=(z-2i)(z+2i).
\]
Its two eigenvalues are exactly $2i$ and $-2i$, both of modulus $2$.
The ratio of largest to smallest eigenvalue modulus is therefore also
$1$, and is again strictly less than the antisymmetric norm $2$.
The witness disproves the lower-bound inequality itself, independently
of the further assertion about attaining the bound.
