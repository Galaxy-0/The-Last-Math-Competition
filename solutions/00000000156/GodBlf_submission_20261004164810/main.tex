\noindent\textbf{Verdict: false.} For every $n\ge3$, the probability in the
conjecture is exactly zero. The general matrix obstruction, the probability
statement, and the failure of the proposed asymptotic are all verified in Lean 4.

\section{Statement and probability model}
The conjecture asserts that the probability that the determinant of a random
$n\times n$ matrix with entries in $\{1,-1\}$ is prime in absolute value is
asymptotic to $c/n$, with an explicit constant $c$. We use the usual uniform
model: the $n^2$ signs are independent and equally likely. Let
\[
 p_n=\frac{\#\{A\in\{1,-1\}^{n\times n}: |\det A|\text{ is prime}\}}
 {2^{n^2}}.
\]
Here primes are positive integers at least 2. The standard meaning of
$p_n\sim c/n$ for $c>0$ is $\lim_{n\to\infty}p_n/(c/n)=1$.

\section{A uniform obstruction}
\begin{theorem}
For every integer $n\ge3$ and every sign matrix $A$ of size $n$,
$4\mid\det A$. Consequently, $|\det A|$ is not prime.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Subtract row 0 from rows 1 and 2. These two row operations preserve the
determinant. Each entry in either resulting row is the difference of two signs,
so it is in $\{-2,0,2\}$. Define integral row vectors
\[
 u_j=(A_{1j}-A_{0j})/2,\qquad v_j=(A_{2j}-A_{0j})/2.
\]
The transformed rows are $2u$ and $2v$. By multilinearity in the rows,
\[
 \det A=4\det C,
\]
where $C$ is the integral matrix obtained by replacing rows 1 and 2 of $A$
with $u$ and $v$. Thus $4\mid\det A$ and $4\mid|\det A|$.
If $|\det A|$ were prime, its divisor 2 would force $|\det A|=2$;
but 4 does not divide 2. This is a contradiction, including the case
$\det A=0$, since zero is not prime.
\end{proof}
Subtracting row 0 from \emph{all} other rows also gives the stronger familiar
fact $2^{n-1}\mid\det A$. The Lean proof uses just two rows, since divisibility
by 4 already proves the required obstruction in every dimension $n\ge3$.

\section{Probability and asymptotics}
The theorem makes the event defining $p_n$ empty for every $n\ge3$.
Therefore $p_n=0$ for all $n\ge3$. For every $c>0$, the ratio $p_n/(c/n)$
is eventually identically zero, hence converges to zero and cannot converge
to one. This disproves the conjecture. Choosing $c=0$ cannot rescue a standard
asymptotic equivalence: its comparison function is zero and the usual ratio
definition is undefined. In Lean, real division is total; even with that
convention, the ratio limit is zero for every real $c$.

For context, $p_1=0$ and $p_2=1/2$. Indeed, for
$A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)$,
$\det A=ad-bc$, and the two independent uniform signs $ad$ and $bc$ differ
with probability $1/2$. These small cases are not needed for the disproof.
