\textbf{Theorem.} Every set-valued monotone operator on a real Hilbert
space admits a maximal monotone extension. Maximal elements in the
inclusion order on monotone graphs are exactly maximal monotone operators.
In fact, the proof works in every real inner-product space, without
assuming completeness.

\section*{1. Definitions and scope}
Let $E$ be a real inner-product space. A set-valued operator is a map
$A:E\to\mathcal P(E)$; empty values are allowed. Its graph is
\[
  \gra A=\{(x,u)\in E\times E:u\in A(x)\}.
\]
It is \emph{monotone} when
\[
  \langle x-y,u-v\rangle\geq0
  \quad\text{whenever }u\in A(x),\ v\in A(y).
\]
An operator $B$ extends $A$ when $A(x)\subseteq B(x)$ for all $x$,
equivalently $\gra A\subseteq\gra B$.
An operator is \emph{maximal monotone} if it is monotone and has no proper
monotone graph extension. This is maximality among monotone operators,
not a claim that its values are maximal subsets of $E$.

These are the standard set-valued monotone-operator meanings of the
conjecture. Both the extension assertion and the graph-order assertion
are proved below. No continuity, nonempty-domain, or full-domain
assumption is imposed. The proof is an application of the axiom of
choice through Zorn's lemma and does not assert uniqueness or provide
an algorithm for selecting an extension.

\section*{2. Monotonicity of chain unions}
Call a subset $S\subseteq E\times E$ a monotone graph when every two
of its points satisfy the displayed inner-product inequality. Let
$\mathcal C$ be a family of monotone graphs linearly ordered by inclusion.
Then $U=\bigcup_{S\in\mathcal C}S$ is a monotone graph.

Indeed, choose $(x,u),(y,v)\in U$. There are $S,T\in\mathcal C$ containing
the first and second points, respectively. Since $\mathcal C$ is a chain,
either $S\subseteq T$ or $T\subseteq S$. In the first case both points lie
in $T$, and in the second both lie in $S$. Monotonicity of that graph
gives $\langle x-y,u-v\rangle\geq0$. This proves the claim. For the empty
chain, its union is empty and monotonicity is vacuous.

\section*{3. Existence and maximality}
Fix a monotone operator $A$ and consider the set
\[
  \mathcal M_A=\{S\subseteq E\times E:\gra A\subseteq S,
       \ S\text{ is a monotone graph}\},
\]
ordered by inclusion. It is nonempty because $\gra A\in\mathcal M_A$.
Every nonempty chain in $\mathcal M_A$ has its union as an upper bound:
the union contains $\gra A$ and is monotone by Section 2.
The empty chain has $\gra A$ as an upper bound.
Zorn's lemma therefore supplies a maximal element $M\in\mathcal M_A$.

Define $B:E\to\mathcal P(E)$ by
\[
  B(x)=\{u\in E:(x,u)\in M\}.
\]
Then $\gra B=M$, so $B$ is monotone and extends $A$.
If a monotone operator $C$ extends $B$, then its graph also contains
$\gra A$, hence belongs to $\mathcal M_A$. Maximality of $M$ gives
$\gra C=M=\gra B$, and therefore $C=B$. Thus $B$ is maximal monotone.

For the second assertion, the assignments
\[
  A\longmapsto\gra A,
  \qquad S\longmapsto\bigl[x\mapsto\{u:(x,u)\in S\}\bigr]
\]
are inverse bijections between set-valued maps and subsets of $E\times E$.
They preserve the extension/inclusion order and the defining monotonicity
inequality. Hence a maximal element of the monotone-graph order is
exactly a maximal monotone operator, and conversely.\hfill$\square$
