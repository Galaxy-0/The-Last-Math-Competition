\begin{abstract}
The conjecture predicts distance $n-1$ between an $n$-simplex and the
$\ell_1^n$ unit ball. In dimension two this predicts one. We show that
every affine copy of the planar cross-polytope containing the standard
triangle requires dilation at least two to fit inside a translated copy
of that triangle. The distance is therefore at least two.
\end{abstract}
\section{Bodies and distance convention}
Let
\[
 K=\{(x,y):x\geq0,\ y\geq0,\ x+y\leq1\},\qquad
 B=\{(x,y):|x|+|y|\leq1\}.
\]
$K$ is a two-dimensional simplex and $B$ is the cross-polytope, that is,
the unit ball of $\ell_1^2$. Use the affine convex-body Banach--Mazur
distance
\[
 d_{\rm BM}(K,B)=\inf\{\lambda>0:
 K\subseteq c+T(B)\subseteq d+\lambda K,
 \ c,d\in\mathbb R^2,\ T\in\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb R)\}.
\]
The middle and outer translations are independent. This convention is
equivalent to putting the inner and outer homothetic copies in the usual
common-center form when $\lambda>1$: their homothety center is
$d/(1-\lambda)$. The proof below even allows all positive dilation factors.
The multiplicative convention is the one in which the question states its
numerical bounds; no logarithm of the distance is being used.

\section{A lower bound for every affine image}
\begin{theorem}
If $K\subseteq c+T(B)\subseteq d+\lambda K$ with $\lambda>0$ and $T$
invertible, then $\lambda\geq2$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The middle body is centrally symmetric about $c=(c_1,c_2)$.
Since it contains the three vertices $(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)$, it also contains
their reflections
\[
 (2c_1,2c_2),\quad (2c_1-1,2c_2),\quad (2c_1,2c_2-1).
\]
Membership of $(x,y)$ in $d+\lambda K$ is exactly
$x\geq d_1$, $y\geq d_2$, and $x+y\leq\lambda+d_1+d_2$.
Applying the sum inequality to the first reflected vertex, the first
coordinate inequality to the second, and the second coordinate inequality
to the third gives
\[
 2c_1+2c_2\leq\lambda+d_1+d_2,
 \qquad d_1\leq2c_1-1,\qquad d_2\leq2c_2-1.
\]
Combining these three inequalities gives $2\leq\lambda$.
\end{proof}

\newpage
The admissible set is nonempty, so taking its infimum is legitimate.
For example, choose $T=I$, $c=(1/3,1/3)$, $d=(-2/3,-2/3)$, and
$\lambda=4$. Each vertex of $K$ lies in $c+B$; convexity gives
$K\subseteq c+B$. For any $q\in B$, the coordinates of $c+q$ are at least
$-2/3$ and its coordinate sum is at most $5/3<8/3$, giving the outer
inclusion. Thus the distance lies in $[2,4]$, which already contradicts
the claimed value $2-1=1$. An exact optimal upper bound is unnecessary.
This refutes the statement in a nondegenerate two-dimensional case,
without relying on the one-dimensional endpoint. It does not decide a
modified conjecture restricted to higher dimensions or an asymptotic claim.

\section{Formal verification and semantic correspondence}
The Lean plane is $\mathbb R\times\mathbb R$.
\texttt{diamond} and \texttt{triangle} encode $B$ and $K$ by their defining
inequalities. \texttt{affineDiamond} uses a real linear equivalence, so it
ranges over every invertible real linear transformation and every center.
\texttt{outerTriangle} is exactly $d+\lambda K$ for $\lambda>0$:
subtract $d$ and divide both coordinates by $\lambda$ to obtain a point
of $K$; \texttt{outerTriangle\_iff} formally verifies this equivalence.
\texttt{Admissible} encodes precisely the two inclusions above,
with independent translations.

\texttt{admissible\_lower\_bound} proves the universal geometric bound;
\texttt{four\_admissible} proves nonemptiness with all inclusions checked
pointwise. The real infimum in \texttt{distance} is bounded below by two
in \texttt{distance\_lower\_bound}, and \texttt{conjecture\_false} refutes
the claimed value one. This is an infimum proof, not merely a failure to
attain dilation one.
